# NIH significance patterns: what funded grants look like

*Last updated: 2026-10-20*

## Synthesis

NIH grant applications — particularly R01s — have a recognizable
structural anatomy that has crystallized over decades of practitioner
experience and is reinforced by the agency's own review criteria.
The most studied single artifact is the **Specific Aims page**: a
one-page document that, by near-unanimous practitioner consensus,
determines a substantial portion of the application's fate before
reviewers read anything else. Empirical work on how reviewers
allocate criterion scores (Eblen et al. 2016) supports this
practitioner intuition: under the legacy five-criterion scoring, the
*Approach* criterion and, to a lesser extent, the *Significance*
criterion were the main predictors of the overall impact score and
the funding outcome.

The 2024 transition to the NIH Simplified Review Framework
restructured how these criteria are scored without removing them.
Reviewers now assess three factors: **Importance of the Research**
(Significance + Innovation, scored 1–9), **Rigor and Feasibility**
(Approach, scored 1–9), and **Expertise and Resources**
(Investigator + Environment, evaluated as sufficient/not sufficient
rather than numerically scored). The five regulatory criteria persist;
their packaging into factors changes the rhetorical work the
Specific Aims page must do. NIH's 2025 scoring guidance for the
framework goes further than a repackaging: it tells reviewers that
the Factor 1 score "should set a limit for the best possible overall
impact score", and that a strong approach cannot lift a project of
moderate importance.[^6] Under that guidance, Importance becomes a
ceiling and Approach can only hold or lower the score. The Eblen
finding (Approach as the strongest predictor) comes from the legacy
scoring regime and may not carry over unchanged.

Funding decisions have also changed downstream of review. Since the
January 2026 Council round, NIH institutes no longer rely on
paylines when deciding what to fund; they weigh peer-review
information "in its entirety" alongside institute priorities and
budgets.[^7] In August 2026 NIH asked for comment on a proposal to
stop releasing numerical overall impact scores and instead report
each application as "Most Competitive" (top 25%), "Competitive"
(26–50%), or "Not Discussed".[^8] NIH has also told applicants that
applications "substantially developed by AI", or containing sections
substantially developed by AI, will not be considered the
applicants' original ideas.[^9]

For scriptorium grant skills, this means three things. First, the
Specific Aims page is the right unit of focus — it is structured,
short, high-leverage, and follows a near-canonical four-paragraph
shape. Second, "what makes a good aims page" is a mix of *practitioner
wisdom* (well-documented across multiple workbooks but mostly
non-empirical) and *empirical findings* (criterion-score
correlations, percentile-funding relationships). The two should be
labeled distinctly. Third, for NIH targets, any skill that produces
aims-page prose must stay inside NIH's originality policy. The
declared-scaffolding scope already points in that direction, but the
policy makes it binding.

## Evidence and frameworks

**Eblen et al. (2016), *PLOS ONE* 11(6):e0155060.** Analyzed the
relationship of the five criterion scores (Significance, Investigator,
Innovation, Approach, Environment) to the Overall Impact score and to
funding outcomes for **over 123,700 competing R01 applications** for
fiscal years 2010–2013. The headline finding, in the abstract's
words: "the Approach and, to a lesser extent, the Significance
criterion scores were the main predictors of an R01 application's
Overall Impact score and its likelihood of being funded." Innovation,
Investigator, and Environment were not named as main predictors. This
corroborates the practitioner adage that a fundable aims page must
convey not just that the science matters (Significance) but that the
work *can be done as proposed* (Approach). The data predate the
Simplified Review Framework.[^1]

**NIH Simplified Peer Review Framework (NOT-OD-24-010, effective
January 2025).** Reorganizes the five criteria into three factors:

- **Factor 1: Importance of the Research** — combines Significance
  and Innovation. Scored 1–9.
- **Factor 2: Rigor and Feasibility** — Approach. Scored 1–9.
- **Factor 3: Expertise and Resources** — combines Investigator and
  Environment. Evaluated as sufficient / not sufficient.

The scale remains 1 (exceptional) to 9 (poor), with 5 as average.
Overall Impact remains a separate 1–9 score that reviewers assign
holistically.[^2] It applies to applications due on or after January
25, 2025 across most research project grant activity codes (R01,
R03, R15, R21, U01, and others). NIH says it adopted the framework
partly to address "the potential for reputational bias to affect
peer review outcomes", and that the binary Factor 3 rating "is
intended to reduce the potential for general scientific reputation
to have an undue influence."[^2] The Significance criterion under
the framework asks reviewers to "assess whether the application
addresses an important gap in knowledge in the field, would solve a
critical problem, or create a valuable conceptual or technical
advance", and to evaluate "the rigor of the scientific background
for the work". The Innovation criterion notes that "a project that
is not applying novel concepts or approaches may be of critical
importance for the field."[^2]

**NIH Simplified Review Scoring Guidance (released March 14, 2025;
effective October 2025 Council round).** Tells reviewers how the
factors combine into Overall Impact: "Logically, a project of
moderate or limited importance (Factor 1) cannot be made more
impactful by a strong approach (Factor 2) and/or appropriate
expertise and resources (Factor 3)"; "Your Factor 1 score should set
a limit for the best possible overall impact score"; and "Your
assessment of Factors 2 and 3 can reinforce this score or worsen
it."[^6] NIH's framework page also says that "an application does not
need to be strong in all categories to be judged likely to have major
scientific impact".[^2] The two statements sit in some tension; the
scoring guidance is the more specific instruction to reviewers.

**NIH Specific Aims structural conventions.** Practitioner
literature converges on a four-paragraph aims page:

1. **Hook / significance opener.** Establishes the problem and its
   scientific or clinical importance. Usually 3–5 sentences ending
   with the gap.
2. **Gap and rationale.** Articulates what is not known, why the
   gap matters, and what the field's working hypothesis is.
3. **Premise and central hypothesis.** Names the long-term goal, the
   immediate objective, and the central hypothesis the proposal
   tests, anchored in the applicant's own preliminary data.
4. **Aims block + impact close.** Two or three specific aims (often
   bulleted), each a 2–4 sentence description of approach and
   expected result. Closes with a sentence on how the work advances
   the field.[^3]

Practitioner sources (NIAID's draft-specific-aims guidance, NINDS's
writing-specific-aims page, the Russell & Morrison *Workbook*) all
prescribe variants of this structure. The convergence is striking
and suggests that *form has won*: applicants who deviate substantially
from this shape pay a legibility cost with reviewers who have
internalized the convention.

**Russell & Morrison, *The Grant Application Writer's Workbook* (NIH
version; Grant Writers' Seminars & Workshops).** The dominant
practitioner workbook for NIH grant writing. Provides a step-by-step
"bulleted outline" approach to the Specific Aims page: significance
of the problem → gap in knowledge → long-term goal → objective →
central hypothesis → rationale → specific aims → expected outcomes →
impact. The workbook is regularly updated to track NIH form changes
(current edition updated January 2026 for FORMS-I).[^4]

**Fang, Bowen & Casadevall (2016), *eLife* 5:e13323.** "NIH peer
review percentile scores are poorly predictive of grant productivity."
A re-analysis of a previously published dataset of NIH R01 grants
funded 1980–2008, restricted to the **102,740 funded grants** with
percentile scores of 20 or better. Within that range, percentile rank
had AUC = 0.54 (95% CI 0.53–0.54) for predicting above-median
citation productivity — essentially equivalent to chance.
The implication: *within the funded range*, peer review cannot
reliably discriminate which grants will be most productive. The
predictive validity of peer review for funding outcomes is real but
constrained; once an application is good enough to be discussed, the
noise floor is high.[^5]

**Eblen et al. extension on triage.** Combining the above with the
NIH triage process: in normal operation CSR states that
"applications in the lower half are not typically discussed", and
streamlined applications receive no overall impact score.[^11] Among
the discussed applications, percentile rank has historically
predicted funding well at the percentile-payline boundary but
predicts productivity weakly within the funded range. Practitioner
implication: the aims page's job is not to optimize for being "the
best" — it is to get *out of the streamlined bottom half* with
enough strength to be competitive. The older framing of this goal as
landing "at or below the percentile payline" no longer describes NIH
practice: institutes stopped relying on paylines from the January
2026 Council round,[^7] and the August 2026 proposal would replace
released scores and percentiles with two discussed tiers.[^8]

**Emergency review modifications (January 2026 through January 2027
Council rounds).** After the October–November 2025 lapse in
appropriations, CSR changed how much of each meeting is discussed:
"The top third (30 to 35 percent) will be discussed in the review
meeting and considered for funding. The middle third will be
designated as 'competitive but not discussed' and considered for
funding. The lowest third will be designated as 'not competitive and
not discussed.'" Summary statements were shortened to a consensus
sentence, bullets on the main score-driving points, and the assigned
reviewers' written critiques. NIH extended these modifications
through the January 2027 Council round after receiving more than
40,000 applications for that round.[^12] So for applications reviewed
in this period, the discussion line sits near the top third rather
than the top half. Applications below it can still be funded if they
fall in the "competitive" middle third. For those applications there
is no discussion, so the written critiques carry most of the case
(researcher inference; the shortened summary statement also has a
consensus sentence and score-driving bullets).

**NIH Unified Funding Strategy (announced November 21, 2025;
effective January 2026 Council round).** NIH states: "NIH ICOs will
not rely on funding paylines in developing pay plans. Rather, ICOs
will consider these scores in context of their and NIH's priorities,
strategic plans, and budgets." Funding decisions "will still weigh
peer review scores and critiques", and institutes are to consider
"peer review information in its entirety", including career stage
and the "broad distribution and geographic balance of funding".[^7]

**Proposed change to reporting review outcomes (NOT-OD-26-088,
request for information; comments closed October 13, 2026).** NIH
proposed that numerical overall impact scores "would no longer be
released to principal investigators, applicant institutions, NIH
program staff, ICO leadership, or NIH Advisory Councils"; applications
would instead be placed in "Most Competitive" (top 25%),
"Competitive" (26–50%), or "Not Discussed". Written critiques,
individual criterion and factor scores, and discussion summaries
would continue to be released.[^8] As of this update it is a
proposal, not adopted policy.

**Centralized peer review.** Effective with the August/October 2025
Council round, all NIH peer review of grants, cooperative agreements,
and contracts is centralized within the Center for Scientific Review
(CSR). NIH states that the review criteria, including the Simplified
Review Framework, do not change.[^10]

**NIH policy on AI-developed applications (NOT-OD-25-132, effective
September 25, 2025 receipt date).** "NIH will not consider
applications that are either substantially developed by AI, or
contain sections substantially developed by AI, to be original ideas
of applicants." NIH says it uses AI-detection technology; if AI use
is identified after award, NIH "may refer the matter to the Office of
Research Integrity" and may disallow costs, suspend, or terminate the
grant. The same notice limits each PI to six new, renewal,
resubmission, or revision applications per calendar year (T activity
codes and R13 conference grants excepted).[^9] The notice does not
define "substantially".

## How this informs scriptorium

**The aims page is a structured artifact, not free prose.** A future
`specific-aims` skill (planned for v0.4 in the [[DESIGN]] roadmap)
should treat the aims page as a structured document with named
slots: hook, gap, premise, hypothesis, aims-list, impact close.
Critique and transformation skills on the aims page should target
specific slots rather than the page as a whole.

**Importance is a ceiling, not one factor among equals.** Under the
2025 scoring guidance, a weak Factor 1 caps the overall impact score
no matter how strong the approach.[^6] Aims-page critique for NIH
targets should therefore check the importance slots (hook, gap,
significance of the expected outcome) *first*, and should not tell
an author that a strong approach section compensates for an
unconvincing gap. This revises the earlier "Approach over
Significance" emphasis drawn from Eblen et al.: that finding still
describes the legacy regime, but for current applications the safer
reading (researcher inference) is "establish Importance, then show
feasibility."

**Criterion-aware critique.** A `grant-reviewer-simulation` skill
should be able to assume reviewer personas that map to the
Simplified Framework factors: an "Importance of the Research"
reviewer focused on whether Significance and Innovation are clearly
articulated; a "Rigor and Feasibility" reviewer focused on Approach
specifics, pitfalls, and alternative strategies; an "Expertise and
Resources" reviewer who issues a sufficient/not-sufficient verdict.
See [[reviewer-archetypes-grants]] for the persona structure.

**No score-to-funding mapping.** Skills must not translate a
simulated or real score into a funding probability or a "below the
payline" verdict. NIH no longer relies on paylines,[^7] and may stop
releasing scores at all.[^8] Critiques should name the strengths and
weaknesses reviewers are likely to write down, because those are the
part of the review NIH says institutes will weigh. Under the
emergency modifications, the written critiques are the main record
for "competitive but not discussed" applications.[^12]

**Conservative-edit posture is even more important in grant
contexts.** Grant text is highly purpose-built: every sentence in the
aims page is doing rhetorical work. The cost of an unauthorized
rewrite is higher than for a manuscript discussion section, because
the aims page is the document reviewers cite when arguing for or
against funding. `MANUSCRIPT_STATE.yaml` for a grant project should
default to strict preservation constraints; transformation skills
should require explicit invocation.

**NIH's AI-originality policy bounds generation.** NOT-OD-25-132
treats sections "substantially developed by AI" as not the
applicant's original ideas.[^9] A skill that renders aims-page prose
for an NIH application is exposed to that policy even when the
underlying aims were declared by the author. Grant skills targeting
NIH should (a) prefer critique and slot-level suggestions the author
writes in their own words over generated paragraphs; (b) state the
policy in their output when `target_funder` is NIH; and (c) never
present generated prose as ready to submit. This is researcher
inference from the notice text; NIH has not said where its threshold
lies.

**Practitioner wisdom is labeled.** Scriptorium should be clear in
its outputs that structural recommendations on the aims page (the
four-paragraph shape, the bulleted aims with expected outcomes, the
impact close) come from practitioner consensus, not from peer-reviewed
empirical evidence on what wins. The empirical evidence is on
criterion-score correlations, not on rhetorical conventions.

**Verdict:** Yes (v0.4), with a changed scope for NIH targets.

**If Yes:** This note grounds the v0.4 `specific-aims` and
`aims-significance-coherence` skills and the grant personas of
`reviewer-simulation`. Phase: v0.4. Required data:
`project.target_type: grant`, a `target_funder` declaration, the
declared aims, hypotheses and significance, and the existing
methods scaffolding.

Two changes since the May 2026 version of this note:

1. **`aims-significance-coherence` is the safer first deliverable**
   (researcher inference). It is pure critique, so it cannot run into
   NOT-OD-25-132. It also targets the Factor 1 ceiling, the
   highest-leverage check under the 2025 scoring guidance.
2. **`specific-aims` should default to a slot audit plus suggestions
   for NIH targets**, with prose rendering available only on explicit
   request and labeled with the NIH originality policy. Whether full
   prose rendering for NIH applications stays in scope is a roadmap
   decision to flag in the PR; this note does not make it.

## Open questions / weak evidence

- The four-paragraph aims-page convention is well-documented in
  workbooks and institutional guidance but, as far as we have
  located, no large-N empirical study compares aims-page structure
  against funding outcomes. The convention may be a coordination
  equilibrium more than a causal advantage.
- The relative weighting of the criteria varies by study section and
  by program officer culture. Generalizations across NIH institutes
  should be hedged.
- The Simplified Framework is too recent (effective 2025) for outcome
  data; how the factor-based packaging changes review behavior is an
  open empirical question. CSR interviewed study-section chairs about
  review under the framework and published four video excerpts
  (September 2025; collected in an NIH Extramural Nexus post of
  January 14, 2026).[^13] As far as we located, NIH has published no
  quantitative analysis of factor scores against overall impact under
  the new guidance.
- Whether Eblen et al.'s Approach-dominance survives the Factor 1
  ceiling instruction is untested.
- NOT-OD-26-088 is a proposal; whether NIH adopts score-free
  reporting, and in what form, was unknown at this update.
- The emergency review modifications are scheduled to run through the
  January 2027 Council round. Whether the one-third discussion line
  and the "competitive but not discussed" category become permanent
  is unknown.
- NOT-OD-25-132 does not define "substantially developed by AI", and
  NIH has not published how its detection works. How the policy is
  applied in practice is unknown.
