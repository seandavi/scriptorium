# EAL Scholarly Writing: Behavioral Evidence

*Last updated: 2026-10-20*

## Synthesis

The literature on English-as-additional-language (EAL) scholars writing for English-medium publication has three large, partially overlapping findings that are robust enough to inform persona design. First, **EAL manuscripts have "text histories"** — by the time a manuscript reaches a journal, it has typically passed through multiple **literacy brokers** (Lillis & Curry's verbatim term [1]): editors, translators, colleagues, paid services, friends. Persona design that models the EAL author as a solo writer + reviewer interacting in isolation misses the social structure that defines the experience. Second, there is a measurable **asymmetric burden**. Amano et al. (2023) surveyed 908 environmental scientists of 8 nationalities [2]. Non-native English speakers reported paper rejection *because of English writing* **2.5–2.6× as often** as native speakers. They reported being asked to improve their English during revision **12.5× as often**. Early-career researchers from moderate-proficiency nationalities spent a median **51% more time writing** a paper, and researchers from low-proficiency nationalities a median **91% more time reading** one. Third, the **interpretive question** of whether to call this "linguistic injustice" remains genuinely contested between Hyland's "myth of linguistic injustice" position [3] and the Politzer-Ahles et al. and Flowerdew responses [4, 5, 6] — a contested debate that a persona should not collapse to one side.

The deepest empirical finding for persona design is about **voice and stance**, not surface correctness. Hyland's (2005) stance/engagement model [7] and subsequent corpus comparisons consistently find EAL authors using fewer hedges, boosters, self-mentions, and explicit reader-engagement markers than L1 authors. Ivanič (1998) [8] provides the theoretical anchor: this is not just style but **discoursal self** — the projected authorial identity that reviewers in the English-medium publishing community read as confidence, expertise, and community membership. An EAL author may receive a reviewer comment like "the argument lacks confidence" or "voice is impersonal" and have no clear path from comment to revision: these are precisely the comments least amenable to local edits and most threatening to identity.

The practical implication for feedback-generating systems is that **specificity is load-bearing for EAL feedback uptake.** This is a researcher synthesis across the L2 feedback literature, not the finding of a single study. **Specific local edits** are taken up most reliably. **Vague global suggestions** ("restructure the discussion," "make the voice more engaging") are the least actionable and the most affectively costly. (An earlier draft attributed the affective-cost finding to "Habib, Corcoran & Englander 2019". That DOI resolves to a single-author study by Soler [9], and the specific claims could not be checked against its full text; see Open questions.) For a system whose skills emit feedback, this means: the same suggestion expressed as a specific local edit and as a vague global instruction will land very differently on an EAL persona, and the persona will reject (or stall on) the latter even when the suggestion is correct. The AI-polishing era introduces a new tradeoff (Jiang & Hyland 2025 [10]; Amano 2023 [2]; Kusumegi et al. 2025 [20]; Filimonovic et al. 2026 [21]). LLM polishing reduces the surface-correctness penalty and is associated with large output gains for authors who are likely non-native speakers. But it tends to **flatten the voice/stance features Ivanič and Hyland identify as identity-bearing**. Reviewers may also read "ChatGPT style" as a demographic signal (Lepp & Smith 2025 [19]), and AI detectors misclassify non-native writing as machine-generated (Liang et al. 2023 [18]). So aggressive AI assistance may deepen the deeper-layer gap that reviewers misread as weak argument, and it can create new suspicion of its own.

## Evidence and frameworks

### Text histories and literacy brokers

Lillis & Curry (2006) [1] introduce **"literacy brokers"** in the context of an 8+ year text-ethnographic study of 50 psychology/education scholars in Hungary, Slovakia, Spain, and Portugal. They distinguish three sub-categories: **academic brokers, language brokers, and non-professional brokers** (friends, family). The book-length report (Lillis & Curry 2010 [11]) documents how the "text history" of an EAL manuscript — the sequence of brokered interventions before submission — is the substrate from which the published article emerges. Curry & Lillis (2010) [12] adds that **network capital** determines broker access: well-networked EAL scholars get better brokers, doubly advantaging center-affiliated and well-connected authors.

The implication for persona design is structural, not surface: the EAL author's *expectation* is iterative external mediation. A persona that responds to a reviewer comment as a solo author confronting it for the first time misses how the actual EAL writer would seek out, integrate, or resist brokered help on the same comment.

### The linguistic-injustice debate (genuinely contested)

Hyland's (2016) **"Academic publishing and the myth of linguistic injustice"** [3] argues, verbatim, that the "Native vs non-Native polarization" framing is "outmoded" and "demoralizes EAL writers and marginalizes the difficulties experienced by novice L1 English academics." Based on Hong Kong interviews plus literature review, Hyland claims much of what is read as "language" rejection is actually unfamiliarity with disciplinary writing conventions, or research problems caused by "physical, scholarly and financial isolation." This is not a fringe position; it is published in the field's leading journal and has shaped subsequent debate.

The response from Politzer-Ahles, Holliday, Girolamo, Spychalska & Berkson (2016) [4] is the canonical counter: **absence of evidence is not evidence of absence**, and Hyland's focus on submission-stage data ignores the upstream cognitive and time burden. Their formulation: "if two scholars with the same ability level and training work on the same topic," the EAL scholar must work harder to produce the same output. Politzer-Ahles, Girolamo & Ghali (2020) [5] adds experimental support: reviewers shown matched abstracts varying only in surface English features rate the "non-native-like" version more negatively, including on content.

Flowerdew (2019) [6] is an invited position piece in *Language Teaching* that responds directly to Hyland. Flowerdew accepts that EAL and L1 writers share many of the challenges of writing for publication. He argues that "EAL writers have an additional set of linguistic challenges, which do not apply (to such an extent) to L1 writers". He concedes that "there is not systematic empirical evidence for bias" by reviewers. But he argues that requiring one group to write in an additional language means "the whole publication process is biased from the outset." (Corrected 2026-10-20: this paragraph previously attributed a 2023 synthesis to Politzer-Ahles, Girolamo & Ghali under DOI 10.1017/S0261444822000428. That DOI resolves to an unrelated Ushioda article. The *Language Teaching* paper titled "The linguistic disadvantage of scholars who write in English as an additional language: Myth or reality" is this 2019 piece by Flowerdew.)

Amano et al. (2023, *PLOS Biology*) [2] is the most quantitatively load-bearing recent evidence. It surveyed 908 environmental scientists of 8 nationalities (Bangladeshi, Bolivian, British, Japanese, Nepali, Nigerian, Spanish, Ukrainian). Among researchers with one English-language paper, 38.1% and 35.9% of non-native speakers from moderate- and low-proficiency nationalities reported a rejection "due to English writing", against 14.4% of native speakers. That makes language-related rejection 2.5–2.6× more frequent. About 42.5% of non-native speakers reported being "often/most of the time/always" asked to improve their English during revision, against 3.4% of native speakers (12.5×). Early-career researchers from moderate-proficiency nationalities spent a median 50.6% more time writing a paper, and the paper adds that "this disadvantage is not found in those at a later career stage." Researchers from low-proficiency nationalities spent a median 90.8% more time reading one. All of these figures are self-reported survey responses. The burden falls more heavily on early-career researchers: some disadvantages "seemed to disappear in late-career researchers", which the authors suspect "could be due to survivorship bias". Income cuts both ways. Researchers from lower-income nationalities underused professional English editing, which the authors read as a sign that disadvantages "could be amplified by a country's and individual's low income level". But barriers to reading papers, preparing oral presentations and attending conferences "appear to be less severe for those of lower income nationalities", again possibly because of survivorship bias. (Corrected 2026-10-20: an earlier draft described these figures as papers being "2.5× more likely to be rejected". They are self-reported frequencies of *language-related* rejection, not overall rejection rates. The earlier draft also said the effect "disproportionately falls on … lower-income-country scholars", which the paper supports only in part.)

**Honest synthesis:** there is good evidence of a measurable disadvantage in time/burden (Amano 2023) and in reviewer responses to surface features (Politzer-Ahles 2020; Lepp & Smith 2025 [19]); the *interpretive* question of whether to call this "injustice" — and what causal weight to assign to language versus disciplinary conventions versus material conditions — remains contested. A persona built only on the disadvantage frame will be one-sided; a persona built only on the Hyland frame will understate empirically documented friction.

### Voice, stance, and the discoursal self

Hyland (2005) [7] is the framework for analyzing the interactional metadiscourse features that distinguish expert and novice (and L1 and L2) academic writing: **hedges, boosters, self-mention, engagement markers**. Subsequent corpus comparisons consistently find EAL authors use fewer of these interactional markers than L1 authors, especially explicit reader-engagement features. The result is prose that sounds "flat" to L1 reviewers in ways that may be misread as weak argument rather than rhetorical-tradition difference.

Ivanič (1998) [8] is the verbatim source for **"discoursal self"** and the four-part identity scheme: **autobiographical self, discoursal self, authorial self, possibilities for selfhood**. For EAL persona design the discoursal/authorial split is critical: an EAL author may have a strong autobiographical self (real expertise, defensible findings) but struggle to project the *discoursal* self that English-medium reviewers expect.

Canagarajah (2002) [13] provides the **"geopolitics of academic writing"** framing — three layered conventions (textual, social, material/publishing) that disadvantage "periphery" scholars. The material chapter is dated but the core argument — that center conventions are *naturalized* rather than neutral — remains foundational.

Casanave (2002) [14], also cited in [[author-role-evidence]], includes bilingual/multilingual faculty in a Japanese university and stresses that academic writing is rule-shaped but not rule-determined. Useful counter to deficit framings of EAL writers.

### Revision patterns: L1 vs L2

Silva (1993) [15] is the meta-review of 72 empirical studies that established the canonical L1-vs-L2 revision-pattern finding (verbatim): L2 writers "revise at a superficial level, re-read and reflect less on their written text, revise less, and when they do revise, the revision is primarily focused on grammatical correction." They also plan less globally. **Caveat:** this body of work is largely about student writers in classroom contexts; extrapolating to mid-career publishing scholars is a real leap and should be flagged in any persona spec that relies on it. The pattern is plausible for early-career EAL scholars writing in a high-stakes second language, less so for senior EAL scholars who have published extensively in English.

### Feedback uptake and the affective load

Soler (2019) [9] traces the trajectories of six early-stage researchers in Swedish academia, in English linguistics and political science, through narrative interviews. In the abstract's account, the study centres on "access to scholarly networks and to quality publication outlets" as key factors in young scholars' development. It also aims to "problematize the notion of 'privilege' that is customarily attributed to L1 English-speaking scholars." It is one of the few studies that follows scholars' publishing trajectories rather than student writing. (Corrected 2026-10-20: this study was previously attributed to "Habib, Corcoran & Englander". The earlier draft also said reviewers' and editors' comments were the most commonly discussed challenge, that EAL authors were affected by them "more deeply than the content alone explains", and that "harsh feedback from journal peer-reviews" was the load-bearing pain point. These details are not in the abstract. Automated access to the full text was blocked, so they are withdrawn pending a manual check.)

Synthesizing across the broader L2 feedback literature (researcher synthesis; no single source in this note establishes it for publishing scholars): **specific local edits** are the highest-uptake feedback form for EAL writers; **vague global suggestions** are the lowest. The asymmetry is plausibly sharper for EAL than for L1 writers because the cognitive cost of converting "restructure the discussion" into concrete edits is itself higher for someone navigating both the rhetorical task and a second language.

### AI / LLM polishing: a new tradeoff

Jiang & Hyland (2025) [10] compare 3-word lexical bundles in argumentative essays written by British A-level students with essays generated by ChatGPT. ChatGPT essays use fewer bundles, but the bundles are more rigid and formulaic. Noun- and preposition-based bundles are more common in ChatGPT text and serve "abstract descriptions" and "transitional and structuring cues". Student essays show "more epistemic stances and authorial presence". For EAL personas this is the key finding: heavy LLM polishing **flattens precisely the stance/engagement features Ivanič and Hyland identify as identity-bearing**. So aggressive AI assistance may *solve* the surface-correctness penalty Amano (2023) documents at the cost of *deepening* the voice/stance gap that L1 reviewers read as weak argument. (Caveat: the human comparison group is L1 school students, not EAL scholars. Applying the finding to EAL scholars is an inference.)

Since 2023 the evidence on AI and EAL scholars has moved from mostly opinion to several large-scale studies. These sections were added in the 2026-10-20 refresh:

- **Detector bias (Liang et al. 2023)** [18]. Seven widely used GPT detectors were tested on 91 TOEFL essays and 88 US eighth-grade essays. The detectors classified the US essays accurately but "incorrectly labeled more than half of the TOEFL essays as 'AI-generated' (average false-positive rate: 61.3%)". 97.8% of the TOEFL essays were flagged by at least one detector. Enriching the essays' vocabulary cut the false-positive rate to 11.6%. The mechanism is lower text perplexity in non-native writing.
- **Reviewer-side language bias persists after ChatGPT (Lepp & Smith 2025)** [19]. An analysis of almost 80,000 ICLR peer reviews found "significant bias against authors associated with institutions in countries where English is less widely spoken" in critiques of writing clarity. There was "only a muted shift" after ChatGPT's release. In interviews with 14 conference participants, reviewers said they now use ChatGPT "style" and non-linguistic features as indicators of author demographics. Authors described an ongoing need to remove features that might expose their "non-native" status. The setting is computer science, a single venue.
- **Output gains are largest for likely non-native authors (Kusumegi et al. 2025)** [20]. This study covers 2.1M preprints on arXiv, bioRxiv and SSRN. LLM adoption, detected from abstract text, was associated with output increases of 23.7–89.3% depending on field and author background. Gains were largest for authors with Asian names at Asian institutions: 89.3% on bioRxiv and 88.9% on SSRN. For Caucasian-named authors at institutions in English-speaking countries the range was 23.7–46.2%. The study also finds a reversal. In human-written preprints, more complex writing predicts publication. In LLM-assisted preprints, it predicts *lower* publication odds. Polish has stopped signalling quality. The design is observational, not causal. (The overall 23.7–89.3% range was confirmed against the arXiv abstract and Cornell University's press release. The subgroup figures, 89.3% on bioRxiv, 88.9% on SSRN and 23.7–46.2% for Caucasian-named authors, are from the authors' arXiv version, arXiv:2601.13187, and have not been checked against the *Science* version of record.)
- **Convergence toward US scientific English (Filimonovic et al. 2026)** [21]. Across 5.65M Scopus articles from 2021–2024, GenAI-assisted papers from non-English-speaking countries "exhibit statistically significant and increasing convergence toward U.S. scientific English". The effect is strongest for domestic teams from countries linguistically distant from English. It is small in absolute terms: about 0.4 percentage points of embedding similarity by 2024. GenAI use is inferred from lexical markers, which the authors call "only an indirect proxy".
- **How EAL academics say they use AI (Gönülal et al. 2026)** [22]. In a mixed-methods study of 105 non-native English-speaking academics from 25 language backgrounds, participants mainly used AI tools for grammar, style, and translation. They said they kept control of higher-level tasks such as organising the manuscript. Their concerns included inaccurate suggestions, over-reliance, non-disclosure, "homogenization of academic writing", and "new forms of inequality". The sample is small and self-reported.

The equity argument is now partly supported. AI reduces the "language tax" (Kusumegi et al. 2025, observational; Filimonovic et al. 2026). It also introduces new suspicion penalties: detector bias is directly demonstrated (Liang et al. 2023), and reviewer-side style cues are reported in interviews (Lepp & Smith 2025). What is still missing is evidence on *publication outcomes* for EAL authors who use AI versus those who do not, with causal identification. Personas should treat AI-mediated writing as a real and growing dimension of the EAL experience without over-claiming what the evidence supports.

## How this informs scriptorium

This evidence base grounds the EAL author-persona work (#42, #43, #44):

1. **The EAL persona is not solo.** Encode that the persona's expectation is brokered help — colleagues, paid editors, AI tools — and that feedback from scriptorium is one input among many. An EAL persona that responds to every comment as if isolated misses the social structure documented by Lillis & Curry.

2. **Don't collapse the linguistic-injustice debate.** The persona should hold both: real surface-feature disadvantage (Amano 2023; Politzer-Ahles 2020) *and* the warning against treating EAL writers primarily as language-deficit subjects (Hyland 2016). A persona that only complains about reviewer bias misses Hyland's evidence; a persona that only blames their own English misses Politzer-Ahles. The honest persona sits with both.

3. **Specificity of feedback is a load-bearing metric.** Skill outputs benchmarked against an EAL persona should be evaluated partly on whether the feedback is **local-specific** vs **vague-global**. The persona will (correctly, per the evidence) reject vague global suggestions as low-uptake — and that becomes a useful pressure on skill design.

4. **Stance/voice comments require special handling.** Comments about hedging, boosters, self-mention, or reader engagement are the comments least amenable to local edits and most identity-threatening for EAL writers (Ivanič 1998; Hyland 2005). A persona should mark these comments as high-cost-to-act-on, and skills that produce such comments should pair them with concrete sentence-level rewrites rather than leaving them as global instructions.

5. **The AI-polishing tradeoff should be encoded.** An EAL persona is plausibly already using ChatGPT, DeepL Write, or Paperpal. Feedback that conflicts with what their LLM tool produced creates friction the persona should register. And feedback that pushes the writing further toward AI-flattened style (more "organizing devices," fewer self-mentions) should be flagged by the persona as deepening the voice problem, not solving it. The 2023–2026 evidence adds two specific risks. First, AI-shaped prose can itself be read as a demographic or AI-use signal by reviewers and detectors [18, 19]. Second, linguistic polish no longer signals quality to reviewers in the way it did [20]. An EAL persona should therefore value voice-preserving edits over fluency-maximising ones. This also supports scriptorium's existing preserve-voice posture (see [[semantic-preservation]] and [[declared-work-scope]]).

6. **Career stage compounds with EAL status, doesn't substitute for it.** The EAL grad-student persona and the EAL senior-PI persona are different cells in the role × language matrix. A senior EAL PI has decades of brokered experience and a polished stance repertoire; a first-time-publishing EAL grad student does not. Persona design should compose the [[author-role-evidence]] findings *with* this document, not pick one or the other.

## Implementation priority for scriptorium

**Verdict:** Yes (knowledge layer for v0.2 persona docs and v0.3 personalization skills). No (not a skill itself).

**Yes — grounding layer:** This document grounds the EAL persona work and the role × language matrix raised in #42 discussion. The EAL persona specs (which live outside the repo per the testing-integrity design) should cite this document for each behavior they model.

**No — dedicated skill:** An `eal-aware-rewriter` skill is *not* justified at this stage. The Amano (2023) burden is real, but the evidence does not yet support a single transformation skill that reliably reduces it. The contested Hyland/Politzer-Ahles debate also cautions against baking a deficit-correction model into a skill. The 2023–2026 AI-era evidence strengthens this verdict. A fluency-maximising rewriter is exactly the tool that converges prose toward a generic style [21], may trigger detector and reviewer suspicion [18, 19], and erodes the signalling value of polish [20].

**If Maybe later:** if persona-calibration data (#44) consistently shows that EAL personas mark certain skill outputs as low-uptake or identity-threatening, that becomes a calibration signal for `argumentative-flow` (#7) and any future drafting skills — pressure to produce more specific, more local feedback. An explicit EAL-aware skill becomes justified if and when the persona-driven evaluation surfaces a clear, repeated pattern that a dedicated skill could address.

## Open questions / weak evidence

- **The Hyland vs Politzer-Ahles/Amano debate is genuinely unresolved.** Personas built one-sidedly will be wrong. The empirical work supports a real surface-feature and time-burden disadvantage; the interpretive question of how much of "language rejection" is actually disciplinary-convention or research-quality rejection remains live.

- **Scholarly EAL writing research is much smaller than student L2 writing research.** Silva (1993) and most revision-pattern studies are about classroom writing; extrapolating to mid-career publishing scholars is a real leap. Personas should be more cautious about Silva-style claims for senior EAL scholars than for early-career ones.

- **Empirical work on EAL scholar feedback-uptake patterns is thin.** Most "feedback uptake" research is on students. Soler (2019) [9] is one of the few studies looking at scholars' trajectories specifically, and it is narrative, not corpus-based. The feedback-specific claims an earlier draft attributed to it could not be verified against the full text in the 2026-10-20 refresh, because the publisher blocked automated access. They need a manual read before they can be restored.

- **Cultural/geographic concentration of the foundational corpus.** Lillis/Curry (Hungary/Slovakia/Spain/Portugal psychology), Flowerdew (Hong Kong Cantonese), Canagarajah (Sri Lanka). Latin American (Englander, Corcoran), African, and South Asian contexts are less represented in the high-citation foundational work, though Corcoran et al. (2019) [16] is correcting this.

- **Literacy-brokers research describes who brokers are but not what they do at the sentence level.** Lillis & Curry document the social structure; the micro-edit patterns brokers apply are less catalogued. This is a real gap for scriptorium because the system is itself effectively a literacy broker.

- **"Discoursal positioning differs by L1 rhetorical tradition" is more often asserted than empirically demonstrated for academic publishing specifically.** Contrastive-rhetoric claims in this literature should be cited with care.

- **AI-use literature for EAL scholars has matured but remains mostly observational.** The large-scale studies (Kusumegi et al. 2025 [20]; Filimonovic et al. 2026 [21]) infer AI use from text and author nationality from names or affiliations. Neither identifies causal effects or individual-level language status. Detector bias (Liang et al. 2023 [18]) is the most direct experimental evidence. The reviewer-cue finding (Lepp & Smith 2025 [19]) comes from a single venue plus 14 interviews. Jiang & Hyland (2025) [10] remain the strongest corpus anchor for stance flattening, but their human comparison group is L1 students.

- **A small number of pagination/volume details in the references list were not pinned at write-time;** the substance of those entries (authors, year, journal, argument) is correct, but exact page ranges should be verified at the source if scriptorium prose ever cites a specific page. Until then, these are treated as work-level rather than page-level citations. (2026-10-20: refs 6, 8, 9, 10 and 11 were corrected against Crossref and Open Library. See the per-entry notes.)
